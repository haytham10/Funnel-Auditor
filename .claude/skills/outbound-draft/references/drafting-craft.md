# Drafting craft — how an abstract line becomes one that lands

Read this when a beat is true but limp. Source: Harry Dry.

_(Examples rewritten 2026-07-31. The rules are unchanged; every worked example
used to sharpen a funnel-audit finding, which taught the exact language
`mechanics.md` now bans. The rules were never about the finding — they are
about turning abstraction into something a reader can see, so they apply to a
hook, an identity beat, the offer and the close.)_

## The three rules (also the core of the gate)

**1. Can I visualize it?** If you can't picture it, they won't remember it. "ten
names sitting in a doc with your name on it" — you can see that. "a curated
prospect list tailored to your business" — you can't see anything.

**2. Can I falsify it?** Provably true or false makes them sit up. "a health
coach in Dubai closed AED 78k over 2 months from prospects I put in front of
them" — checkable, sharp. "I get coaches great results" — nobody can argue, so
nobody cares.

**3. Can nobody else say it?** Could another freelancer send this exact email to
another coach with the name swapped? If yes, it's a template and it's dead. The
hook must be bespoke to them.

## Make it concrete (zoom in)

Take the vague word and rewrite until you hit something you can see.
- Abstract: "I can help you find clients"
- Zoom: I find people who'd buy from you
- Zoom: I've already found some
- Concrete: "I pulled 10 names for you before writing this"

Never send the abstract version.

## Make it falsifiable (point, don't talk)

You can't talk, you can only point. No adjectives doing the work — no
"amazing," "powerful," "incredible." Point at the exact thing they said, on the
exact date.
- Talking: "loved your recent content on burnout"
- Pointing: "your episode on eldest kids, the bit about the 2 choices framework"

A falsifiable line is you putting your head on the chopping block. That is what
makes them read it.

## Make it bespoke (look deeper)

If the line could describe 500 coaches, you haven't looked hard enough. If you
could paste it into another coach's email, rewrite it until you can't.

## Conflict — put it in every email

We judge relatively. Two things next to each other sharpen each other. Write it
with a hidden "but": the thing was going one way, but. "10 names, and I'll tell
you why these 10 and not the other 40" — the 40 is what makes the 10 mean
something.

Note what this is NOT any more: it used to mean showing them a problem. Naming
what is wrong with their business is the dead offer, and `critical-failures.md`
bans it outright. The contrast now runs between what you did and what you left
out, never between what they have and what they lack.

## Facts — if in doubt, give a fact

A fact guarantees you've said something. Most outreach is word-shaped air.
Never "I help coaches get more clients." Always the specific number, the
specific person, the specific thing. "6 meetings for an executive coach here,
and I'm still working with them." Point at the graph, don't describe it.

Every number traces to `copy/results.csv`, and a number next to a named segment
has to belong to that segment. `python main.py lint` fails closed on both.

## Small numbers, bigger timeframe

The big number lands; the daily one doesn't. "AED 400k in signed business for
the coaches I worked with this year" beats the same result described one client
at a time.

## The quality bar

- 9/10 or it doesn't go.
- The One Mississippi test: does the line land in two seconds? Run subject lines
  through this hardest.
- The garden-walk test: done when you walk away, come back, and can't add or
  remove a word.
